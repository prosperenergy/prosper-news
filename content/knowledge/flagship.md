I put my first solar system on a roof in Charleston, South Carolina. Since then, my team has put up more than 24,000 of them across 42 states. I have sat at a lot of kitchen tables, and I have listened to a lot of people in nice suits explain the next big thing in energy.

Right now, that next big thing is mini nuclear. Small modular reactors. You have seen the headlines. Here is my position: **a family should not postpone a practical solar and battery decision because somebody announced a reactor.**

A nuclear announcement cannot lower your bill today. A reactor investment is not backup power in your garage. And an ambitious cost target is not a price anyone has demonstrated on your electric bill.

## The pitch

Build more of the reactor in a factory. Standardize the design. Assemble smaller units. Repeat the process until construction gets faster and costs come down.

That is a legitimate engineering ambition. It is also the part of the sales pitch that deserves the hardest questions. How much does the first completed project cost? When does it deliver? Who pays if the schedule slips? How many repeat orders are needed before the hoped-for savings arrive?

I sell solar and batteries. You should know that before reading my argument. I still expect the evidence to carry the argument. My business interest does not turn a projection into a fact, and neither does a reactor developer's.

## Promised versus what actually happened

**Vogtle, Georgia — full-size reactors, not SMRs.** Original estimate: about $14 billion. Final cost: more than $30 billion. The U.S. Energy Information Administration reports that the two new units were initially expected in 2016 and 2017. They entered commercial operation in 2023 and 2024. Those completed reactors now generate substantial low-carbon electricity. Their construction history still demonstrates how badly a nuclear capital project can outrun its original budget and schedule. [EIA record](source:vogtle)

**NuScale's Idaho project — an actual SMR development.** Earlier target: $58 per megawatt-hour. Updated target in January 2023: $89 per megawatt-hour. Outcome: cancelled in November 2023. These were project price targets with financial and subsidy assumptions, not bills from an operating plant. NuScale and its utility partner ended the Carbon Free Power Project after failing to secure enough customer participation. The later published schedule had aimed for a first module in 2029 and full operation in 2030. It was not a plant promised to be operating in 2026. The decisive fact is simpler: the flagship project did not reach construction and commercial delivery. [Earlier utility account](source:nuscale-early) · [Updated target](source:nuscale-price) · [Termination](source:nuscale-cancel)

**Darlington, Ontario — an approved construction program, not an operating cost result.** First reactor and shared infrastructure: C$7.7 billion. Four-reactor program: C$20.9 billion. The project description separates approximately C$6.1 billion for the first reactor from C$1.6 billion for infrastructure shared by the four-unit site. That distinction matters. Comparing the whole financed Canadian program against an early vendor estimate in another currency does not establish a clean tenfold cost overrun. The documented budget is a large commitment on its own. It now has to become operating performance. OPG targets the first unit's grid connection for the end of 2030. [Government project record](source:canada) · [OPG budget and schedule](source:darlington)

**V.C. Summer belongs in this history too:** its two additional large reactors were abandoned in 2017. That does not mean the site's existing operating reactor produced no power. It also does not justify claiming the unfinished units can never be revisited. Santee Cooper and Brookfield are studying whether completion is feasible. A feasibility study is another decision to watch, not electricity delivered. [Current feasibility program](source:summer)

And no, it is not accurate to say no SMR has ever worked anywhere. The IAEA documents operating examples in Russia and China. That establishes technical reality. It does not establish the delivered price of a proposed North American project or provide an American family with backup power. [Operating examples](source:smr-operating)

## Follow the money, then ask for delivery

Big Tech's interest is real. Meta's January 2026 announcement described arrangements supporting up to 6.6 gigawatts of new and existing nuclear capacity by 2035. That includes keeping existing plants operating as well as developing new reactors. Treating all of it as near-term SMR output would mislead readers. [Meta announcement](source:meta)

I understand why a company operating enormous data centers wants firm power. Its procurement problem is different from a homeowner's. A technology company can pursue a portfolio of long-term contracts. A family trying to manage this summer's electricity bill needs an option that can actually be assessed and installed at its property.

The International Energy Agency models substantial possible SMR deployment. Those scenarios depend on policy, financing, execution and cost reductions. They are evidence that the industry could develop, not proof that its cheapest advertised future power is already available. [IEA analysis](source:iea-nuclear)

My objection is to selling that future as a reason for families to wait.

## The math that belongs on the table

Smaller reactors can reduce the size of an individual investment. They do not automatically reduce the cost of each unit of electricity. Factory production and repeat construction might bring savings. Losing economies of scale can push the other way. Financing and a long construction schedule can swamp a promising engineering estimate.

Solar has something more tangible to show: a large deployed market and an established cost history. IRENA's 2026 report puts the global weighted average cost of electricity from newly commissioned utility-scale solar PV in 2025 at **$44 per megawatt-hour, 89 percent below 2010**. It was broadly flat against 2024; the record does not support saying costs fall every single year. [IRENA cost report](source:irena)

That is utility-scale electricity, not a rooftop quote. It is also a generation cost, not the cost of providing firm electricity every hour. Storage, transmission, balancing and other system needs have to be included when comparing complete power systems.

Even with those distinctions, the direction is powerful. Solar has an operating market behind its progress. SMRs still have to demonstrate that their proposed factory savings can survive commercial delivery at scale.

Research by Ziegler and Trancik found a roughly 97 percent decline in the real price of lithium-ion cells from 1991 to 2018. That is a historical cell-price result, not a promise that an installed home battery falls by the same amount. Their work and subsequent research on the drivers of battery cost reductions show why sustained research, manufacturing improvements and deployment matter. [Cost history](source:battery-history) · [Cost-reduction drivers](source:battery-drivers)

I will take a technology with an extensive record of learning over a brochure that assumes learning has already happened.

## What if half of existing solar homes added batteries?

This is the question I want on the table. Not just what we might build from scratch, but what we can do with the roofs families are already paying for.

Using six million U.S. solar installations and a 97 percent residential share gives an approximate base of 5.82 million residential installations. If half of that base added one 13.5-kilowatt-hour battery, it would mean **2.91 million new batteries and about 39.3 gigawatt-hours of usable storage**. That is a scenario, not a count of orders. Residential installations are a household proxy, and we have not subtracted systems that already have batteries. [SEIA installation data](source:seia)

At a published installed-price benchmark of $15,647 per battery, the equipment-and-installation illustration totals about **$45.5 billion before incentives**. It does not include the cost of the solar already installed. It is not a Prosper quote or a complete national program budget. A three-year rollout would require about 80,833 installations a month. That is the pace required, not a promise that today's industry can achieve it. [Battery-price benchmark](source:battery-price)

Storage is not generation. We cannot call those batteries a direct replacement for a nuclear fleet that can keep producing electricity. We can say they would represent a substantial pool of time-shifting and potential local backup capacity, if designed and operated for those jobs.

The full comparison, currency conversion, deployment pace and emissions assumptions are public. **[Change the numbers yourself in the national storage calculator](/guides/half-of-solar-homes/).**

## The wire is part of the problem

Berkeley Lab reports about 2,060 gigawatts of proposed generation and storage in U.S. interconnection queues at the end of 2025, including 773 gigawatts of solar and 749 gigawatts of battery storage. For regions with available data, projects completed in 2025 had a median wait exceeding five years. [Berkeley Lab and GridTracker data](source:queues)

Those are proposed projects. They are not all built, financed or ready for construction. The battery figure describes power capacity, not how much energy the batteries can deliver or for how long.

The practical point survives those corrections: building generation is only part of delivering usable power. A new reactor also needs an appropriate site and a way to serve its load. Announcing it does not remove network constraints.

A rooftop system can generate electricity at the property where some of it is consumed. A battery can move a portion of that electricity into hours when the home needs it. Most residential systems do not join the wholesale transmission queue described in Berkeley Lab's report.

They still need applicable permits, inspection and utility approval. Some projects need service or network upgrades. Anyone telling you a grid-connected home system never needs permission is overselling it. [DOE permitting guidance](source:permits)

The advantage is local generation and a smaller project scope. That is enough to make a strong case without pretending the rules disappear.

## The next fight is over control

Solar puts generation on your roof. Batteries add the ability to decide when some of that energy gets used. Connect many systems through an aggregator and they can participate in coordinated grid services. This is a virtual power plant: physical devices, connected through software, delivering a service together. [DOE explanation](source:vpp)

That changes the business. My read is that the next major contest is not simply who installs the panel. It is who coordinates the power, controls dispatch and keeps the value. Aggregation is not the same thing as owning the utility's distribution wires or becoming a licensed electricity retailer.

It is a large economic arena. The IEA estimates global household electricity spending exceeded $1 trillion in 2024. That is spending on electricity, not a revenue forecast for home batteries or for Prosper. [IEA spending analysis](source:spending)

If someone wants your battery in a network, ask what stays reserved for your family, what you get paid, who controls charging and export, and how you leave. A lease, a loan, a battery warranty and a grid-services agreement are not interchangeable documents.

**Your roof should not become somebody else's power asset without you understanding the deal.** [Read the homeowner control checklist](/guides/who-controls-your-power/).

## Tell the human cost straight

Nuclear power is a low-carbon energy source. That fact belongs in an honest comparison. So do its risks, waste obligations and history.

Chernobyl caused acute radiation deaths, increased thyroid cancer among people exposed young, and immense disruption. The widely cited WHO and Chernobyl Forum figure of up to 4,000 eventual deaths among the most exposed groups is a projection, not a count of individually observed deaths. [WHO assessment](source:chernobyl)

Fukushima requires different language. UNSCEAR says no adverse health effects among residents have been documented as directly attributable to radiation exposure from the accident, and a detectable increase is not expected. Evacuation, displacement and psychological distress caused serious harm. Quoting speculative cancer estimates as an established death toll would obscure that distinction. [UNSCEAR findings](source:fukushima)

The uranium legacy also deserves attention. EPA documents more than 500 abandoned uranium mines on and near the Navajo Nation. That history includes uranium production associated with the broader weapons and civilian nuclear era; it cannot all be assigned to today's proposed reactors. It is still a reminder that an energy price can leave long-lived obligations behind. [EPA cleanup record](source:uranium)

I do not need to exaggerate cancer claims or wildlife damage to question a new nuclear project's economics. My argument is about delivery, cost, accountability and the choices families can make now. Our [health evidence review](/guides/nuclear-health-evidence/) includes both concerning studies and findings that do not support a blanket cancer claim.

## What actually works for a home

A home solar and battery proposal can be evaluated with information a family already understands: the roof, the electric bill, the loads that matter during an outage, the financing terms and the utility's rules.

A battery does not promise unlimited backup. Its usable energy, output power, state of charge, protected circuits and the outage conditions determine what it can run and for how long. Solar equipment also needs the right controls to operate safely during an outage. Critical medical equipment needs a backup plan based on its actual requirements. [DOE storage basics](source:storage-basics) · [Rooftop resilience](source:backup)

For the right home, that system can reduce purchases from the grid, improve control over when electricity is used, and support selected loads when utility service fails. It cannot erase every utility charge, make shade disappear or turn an unfavorable loan into a good deal.

If you already have solar, start by checking production. A battery does not repair a failed inverter. People still making their solar payments need that system working **now**. [Use the solar-performance checklist](/guides/solar-performance/).

Those are reasons to demand a clear proposal. They are not reasons to wait for a reactor press conference.

## The line in the sand

My position is aggressive because the decision is immediate: families with suitable homes should evaluate solar and batteries now. I expect distributed solar and storage to remain important for decades. Nobody can scientifically guarantee that one configuration will be the best financial choice for every American family for the next 20 or 30 years.

Maybe mini nuclear earns a larger role. If it demonstrates competitive costs and reliable delivery, I will say so. Keeping an existing reactor operating can also be a very different decision from financing a new one.

But an uncertain future project is a weak excuse for ignoring an available household option. Your family does not need to win a national technology argument before looking at its own numbers.

Get your home looked at. Find out what your roof can produce and what a battery would cover. Ask for the complete price, the financing terms and the assumptions behind the savings. Then decide with your own numbers in front of you.
