Lifecycle guide · Health & waste

The obligation does not end when the power is sold.

Nuclear waste, uranium legacies, and why solar and battery recycling also belong in the plan.

The electricity may be sold today. The cleanup obligation can last much longer.

Our standard is straightforward: name the material risks, identify who is responsible and show how the obligation will be funded. That applies to nuclear fuel and uranium sites. It also applies to solar panels and batteries.

Nuclear waste is not just an image problem

Spent nuclear fuel remains radioactive and needs shielding, controlled storage and long-term management. Storage in pools or dry casks is a management method, not a claim that the material has become harmless. NRC high-level waste guidance

The fair questions for a proposed project are specific: what fuel and waste streams does this design create, where will they go, who holds responsibility, and what happens if the operator or financing arrangement changes? “Small” does not answer those questions.

Different reactor designs can produce different waste characteristics. We should not assume that every proposed SMR has the same waste profile, or attach a dramatic waste multiplier without a design-specific study.

The uranium legacy is documented

EPA's cleanup record identifies more than 500 abandoned uranium mines on and near the Navajo Nation. That is an environmental obligation people live with—not a theoretical future issue. EPA cleanup record

The historical uranium economy included weapons and civilian uses. It would be wrong to charge all of that legacy to today's proposed SMRs. It would also be wrong to leave mining and cleanup outside the conversation simply because the power plant is elsewhere.

Ask whether a proposal's sourcing, remediation and financial-responsibility arrangements address the entire fuel pathway.

Ask about water, not just the reactor label

For any thermal power project, request the actual cooling design, site water plan, permitted withdrawals and discharges, drought contingencies and expected performance in extreme conditions. These are due-diligence questions, not a claim that every nuclear design uses the same amount of water.

Separate water withdrawal from water consumption. Do not compare a power plant's cooling withdrawal with a rooftop panel's cleaning water as though that were a complete lifecycle assessment. The unit, boundary and local water conditions must match.

Solar does not get a free pass

Panels eventually need reuse, recycling or disposal. EPA explains that panel composition and waste classification matter, and recycling access and requirements vary. Keep the equipment identity and a credible end-of-life plan rather than assuming “renewable” means “no waste.” EPA solar-panel recycling

Home batteries add electrical and fire hazards that require appropriate equipment, siting and professional installation. Certification and testing are part of safety, not proof that an installation can never fail. UL residential storage guidance

Questions to keep with your proposal

  1. Who owns the equipment and the obligation to remove it?
  2. What is covered by warranty, and for how long?
  3. What happens to damaged, recalled or retired equipment?
  4. Which costs are included now and which are future owner obligations?
  5. Who can service the system if the original installer is unavailable?
  6. Is the end-of-life pathway a named service or just a general aspiration?

Our position

Nuclear's low-carbon generation does not erase its waste and fuel obligations. Solar and storage's practical advantages do not make them impact-free. We favor equipment that can help families now, installed safely and backed by clear service and end-of-life responsibility.

The strongest environmental argument is accountable, not selective.
