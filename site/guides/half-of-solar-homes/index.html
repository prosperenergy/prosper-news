National scenario · Battery storage

What if half of America’s solar homes added storage?

About 39.3 GWh in one explicit scenario. Change the assumptions and check the cost yourself.

Start with the roofs already in place. If half of America's existing residential solar base added one 13.5 kWh battery, this scenario produces about 39.3 GWh of usable storage. That is a substantial opportunity to examine—not a claim that batteries manufacture energy or replace nuclear generation one for one.

The baseline, in plain sight

SEIA's six-million-installation milestone reports a 97 percent residential share. Multiplying gives approximately 5.82 million residential installations. We use installations as a proxy for homes; that is not an exact census of unique homeowners. Installation dataset

The scenario assumes half of that base receives an additional battery. It does not subtract homes already equipped with storage, so it is not the number of retrofits needed to reach 50 percent total battery adoption.

What the baseline means

  • 2.91 million additional batteries.
  • 39.285 GWh of usable storage before any reserved-energy or availability reduction.
  • US$45.533 billion at the benchmark price, excluding the solar already installed.
  • 80,833 installations a month, approximately, to complete that scope in 36 months.

The model is multiplication, not a deployment forecast. It does not independently price program administration, financing, repairs to existing solar, network upgrades, aggregation or replacements. The installed benchmark may include some site work, but it is not a national retrofit bid. Supply chains, workforce, permitting, household participation and interconnection still determine achievable pace.

At the same size and adoption, a US$13,055 price sensitivity gives about US$37.99 billion; US$20,000 gives US$58.2 billion. These are arithmetic sensitivities, not offers available to every home.

Compare capital without pretending the services match

Darlington's four-reactor program budget is C$20.9 billion. At the Bank of Canada's September 11, 2026 rate of C$1.3866 per US$1, that converts to approximately US$15.073 billion. Darlington budget · Exchange-rate observation

At US$15,647 per home, the same dollar amount would buy roughly 963,305 illustrative battery installations, totaling about 13.0 GWh. To equip the full 2.91 million homes for that amount would require about US$5,180 per home, far below the benchmark used here.

So the honest answer is: this half-of-solar-homes scenario does not cost less than Darlington's stated program budget at today's chosen retail benchmark. It is a different investment delivering different services to many properties.

Darlington would generate electricity over a long operating life. Batteries store electricity made elsewhere, lose some in the process and will require replacement over a comparable horizon. A financed nuclear-program budget and a retail installed-battery benchmark are not equivalent lifecycle costs. This is a capital-allocation illustration, not a levelized-cost comparison.

How much power, and for how long?

39.285 GWh divided by four hours is 9.821 GW as an energy-only average-power ceiling. That assumes full starting charge, no household reserve, suitable inverters and simultaneous participation.

If 80 percent of energy is available for dispatch and 80 percent of the fleet participates, the arithmetic falls to 6.286 GW over four hours. Those 80 percent values are assumptions. This is not accredited firm capacity. Systems in different regions cannot automatically relieve the same local network constraint, and household backup commitments limit what can be exported.

Real aggregation is more than a spreadsheet. A Brattle analysis of a July 29, 2025 California test reported about 535 MW average delivery over two hours, based on operator-supplied telemetry. That is evidence of an actual coordinated event, not proof of continuous output or national scalability. Event analysis and limitations

Faster is a deployment question

The required pace would be about 121,250 installations monthly for 24 months; 80,833 for 36; or 60,625 for 48. A hypothetical three-year program starting in September 2026 would finish around September 2029, before OPG's end-2030 first-unit grid-connection target. Neither the hypothetical battery schedule nor a reactor target is an assured completion date. OPG target

Distributed deployment has a useful feature: individual completed systems can begin serving homes while later installations continue. But “millions of systems sooner” requires a funded, staffed and permitted program. It cannot be declared from arithmetic alone.

Greener depends on when the batteries charge

Suppose each new battery delivers 10 kWh on 300 days a year. Across 2.91 million systems that is 8.73 TWh shifted annually. At an assumed 90 percent round-trip efficiency it requires 9.70 TWh of charging energy. It is shifted energy, not new generation.

Illustrative operational carbon arithmetic is:

avoided emissions = discharged energy × displaced emissions rate − charging energy × charging emissions rate.

Charge at 0.3 kg CO₂/kWh; displace 0.4 0.582 million tonnes avoided
Charge and displace at 0.4 0.388 million tonnes added because of losses

These are teaching examples, not a national emissions estimate. They exclude manufacturing and end-of-life impacts. Solar that would otherwise serve the grid is not automatically “free” zero-opportunity-cost charging energy. Research on storage dispatch likewise shows that economic arbitrage does not automatically reduce emissions. Storage-emissions research

The case that survives the math

We already have a large residential solar base. Adding well-designed storage can make some of that generation more useful to homes and, with the right agreements, to the grid. The benefits depend on cost, dispatch, local constraints and homeowner protection.

That is a serious deployment opportunity. It does not need a fake claim that batteries are generators, an unsupported cancer statistic or an impossible price to deserve attention.
