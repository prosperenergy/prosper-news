# The Man Behind the Prosper Method

## Craig Stratton on family, loss, a life in sales, and the responsibility of building something people can depend on

*An interview-style profile for Prosper News, drawn from Craig Stratton’s Built Back Bold, his résumé, recorded conversations, and written statements. The questions and first-person answers are adapted and expanded for this profile; they are not a verbatim transcript of a new interview.*

In the dedication to Built Back Bold, Craig Stratton remembers his father hiding the television remote.

It is a small detail in a book with large ambitions. His father, Dr. Clifford James Stratton, could become absorbed in a textbook, turn dinner into a lesson, and then interrupt the seriousness with a prank. Craig remembers the laughter as clearly as the learning. In the dedication, the title that matters most is Dad.

That is an important place to begin a story about a chief executive.

There are other ways to introduce Stratton: founder of Prosper, sales leader, author, mentor, a businessman whose career has moved through home security, finance, home automation, and solar. His professional history includes APEX Technologies, APX Security Solutions, Edward Jones, and Vivint. His company story reaches from a garage in South Carolina to relationships with owners and operators across the country.

But his book asks readers to understand the person carrying that history. He writes about growing up as the youngest of six, struggling with a stutter, learning responsibility early, and eventually building a career around the very thing that once challenged him: communicating with people.

He also writes about losing four people within less than a year: his sister Eden, his brother Chuck, his father-in-law Carl, and his father. They were part of his daily life, his family, and, in different ways, the foundation of how he worked. Their absence changed his understanding of success and the kind of future he wanted to build.

The business continued to require decisions. People continued to need answers. The person answering them was changing.

That personal history now sits alongside the practical thinking he calls the Prosper Method. In an October 1, 2026 conversation with Nick Tvrdy, Stratton describes a company that combines its own capabilities with those of selected partners: installation, equipment, financing access, sales, and the systems that connect them. He talks about protecting the economics for the rep and the installer, respecting local knowledge, and giving independent businesses more ways to succeed.

It is a business model built around relationships. His book explains why relationships carry so much weight for him.

The conversation below brings those two stories together: the man shaped by the people he loved, and the business he wants to make useful to the people who depend on it.

## Where does your story begin?

**Craig:** I’m originally from Nevada, and I’m the youngest of six. When I write about where I came from, I start with family and responsibility. I helped care for a brother who needed support. I learned to pay attention to what another person needed and to take responsibility before I had much experience doing it.

That is part of the background behind the person people see now. They may know me as a CEO or someone who can get in front of a room and speak. They may not know that I grew up struggling with a severe stutter. Speaking could take real effort. There was hesitation, embarrassment, and the experience of having something to say while struggling to get it out.

I think that matters when you tell someone’s story. Confidence has a history. People see what you can do today without necessarily seeing what it took to become comfortable doing it.

Scouting was another part of my early life. I became an Eagle Scout. Later, I served a mission in Scotland and studied at BYU–Idaho, where I completed a double major in Business Finance and Business Management.

Those experiences gave me different kinds of preparation. Family taught responsibility. Scotland required patience and persistence. Education gave me a way to think about how businesses and financial decisions work. The field gave me the chance to test what I understood with real people.

I have kept building from those experiences. Some of the lessons came through success. Some came through realizing that I needed to listen more carefully, learn something new, or ask someone else for help.

## How did someone who struggled to speak build a career around communication?

**Craig:** In Built Back Bold, I describe what it felt like to struggle with words as a child. That is part of why I want people to understand the person before they look at the accomplishments. A difficulty at the beginning of your life can become part of the work you spend years learning how to do.

My work eventually required me to approach people, explain ideas, answer questions, train teams, and stand in front of a room. Those abilities developed through experience. I had to keep participating in conversations and keep learning from them.

Scotland was important in that development. You could knock on doors, put in the effort, and hear no. You learned to keep your composure and continue. You also learned that there was an actual person on the other side of the door, with a life and a point of view you needed to respect.

My faith belongs in that part of the story. Service asks something of you even when there is no applause and no immediate result. You have to care enough to keep showing up, and you have to remain humble enough to listen.

I carried that experience into sales. Preparation mattered. Practice mattered. So did learning to hear what the other person was actually saying. A conversation becomes more useful when you understand the concern behind the question.

When I work with a newer rep, I remember that people develop at different speeds. Someone may have real ability and still need practice, instruction, and another opportunity. I want to help them become capable enough that their confidence has something underneath it.

The way I communicate today was built over time. I want someone reading this to understand that their current difficulty does not have to be the limit of what they learn to do.

## What did the different chapters of your career teach you?

**Craig:** They gave me different views of the same responsibility: helping a person make a decision and then being accountable for the work behind it.

I came up through residential alarm sales and moved into management and training. You learn the customer conversation yourself, then you become responsible for helping other people learn it. That changes the job. Your own performance matters, and so does your ability to explain what works, recognize where someone is struggling, and build a team that can keep improving.

At Edward Jones, the conversations involved people’s financial goals and their futures. That work required listening carefully to what mattered to the person sitting across from you. Financial language has to become something a person can understand well enough to make a decision about their own life.

At Vivint, I worked in regional sales leadership across multiple states. You deal with production, coaching, recruiting, customer demonstrations, and the practical differences between markets. You learn that an idea has to work through people and processes before it becomes a result.

Prosper brings those experiences together. We need people who can sell, people who can deliver, and an operation that connects the two. We need to understand the economics, explain the opportunity accurately, and help the customer understand the choice.

I am interested in how the whole thing works. If the rep has a good conversation but the project cannot be delivered as represented, there is still a problem. If an installer does excellent work but cannot get a dependable flow of projects, there is an opportunity to improve the arrangement.

That is where a lot of my experience has become useful: seeing the connection between parts of the business that are often discussed separately.

His September 2026 executive résumé places APEX Technologies around 2006–2007, APX Security Solutions in 2008–2009, Edward Jones in 2009–2011, and Vivint in 2011–2016. It dates his current Prosper founder and CEO role from December 2016. In recorded conversations, Stratton traces the earlier business roots to his garage and a 2010 incorporation. These describe different chapters of his business history rather than a single uninterrupted job title.

## Your book begins with your father. What did he give you that still shows up in your work?

**Craig:** Curiosity. The willingness to get interested in something and keep asking questions until you understood it.

Dad could be completely absorbed in learning. Then he would bring what interested him into a conversation with us. In the dedication, I describe family dinners that combined stories, science, laughter, and a lot of personality. He could be serious about an idea without making the room feel stiff.

He had this ability to help you become interested too. That is what I remember about the way he taught. You were allowed to wonder. You could ask a question, discover something, and enjoy the moment when it made sense.

I also remember the humor. The pranks. The laugh. It matters to include those things because people are made up of more than the accomplishments you can fit into a biography. He was a person we loved being around.

I see his influence when I am trying to explain a complicated part of the business. Understanding something yourself is only part of the work. Can you help the next person understand it? Can you make the explanation useful enough that they can do something with it?

That applies to a customer looking at a proposal, a rep learning a process, or an owner trying to work through a difficult decision. The knowledge becomes more valuable when you can share it clearly.

The book is one way I carry that influence forward. I can put what I have learned into words and make it available to someone else. I can also keep his humor and curiosity in the way I live. Those everyday qualities are part of his legacy too.

## What happened in the year that became the heart of Built Back Bold?

**Craig:** Within less than a year, I lost Eden, Chuck, Carl, and my dad. Each of them occupied a different place in my life. Their losses affected my family, my routines, my work, and my understanding of who I was.

Eden was my older sister. In the book, I describe the shock of discovering how ill she was so close to losing her. Chuck was my brother, my friend, and someone who helped build the systems behind my business. Carl was my father-in-law and a mentor. Dad was the person whose curiosity, humor, and way of teaching had been there throughout my life.

When you lose people like that close together, there are practical things to do. There are calls, arrangements, family responsibilities, and work that is still waiting. At the same time, you are trying to understand a life that has changed in ways you did not choose.

I write about the ordinary moments because that is where absence keeps showing up. You want to share something with someone. You reach for your phone. You expect a familiar voice or a familiar response, and then you remember.

Being responsible for a business did not make me exempt from that. It meant I was carrying grief while other people still needed me to make decisions. I had to reconsider what strength looked like and how much I could expect myself to carry alone.

Built Back Bold came from trying to put language around those experiences and what I was learning through them. I wanted to make something useful from what I had lived through while still honoring the people themselves.

I would want a reader to understand that the love comes first. These were people with personalities, stories, humor, and influence. Remembering them should leave room for all of that.

## What did Eden’s life and loss make you reconsider?

**Craig:** Eden was protective. In the book, I describe her wanting to spare the family from worry about her illness. I also describe how painful it was to realize how much I had not known and how little time was left.

It made me think about the burdens people carry while trying to protect everyone around them. Someone can care deeply about you and still find it difficult to tell you what they are going through.

I recognized a question for myself as a leader: when I think I am protecting people by carrying everything privately, am I also keeping them from helping? There are responsibilities that belong to me. There are also situations where sharing the truth gives other people a chance to contribute.

That does not mean every private difficulty belongs in a team meeting. It means I want to be more thoughtful about the assumption that strength requires silence.

With the people around me, I want to pay attention. Ask how they are doing. Give them time to answer. Remember that someone who is dependable and capable may also be having a difficult time.

Those are simple actions, but they ask you to slow down enough to see the person. Eden’s place in my life keeps that question close to me.

## Chuck helped build the business with you. What do you want people to know about him?

**Craig:** He understood systems. He could see how the pieces fit together and help make complicated things work. That contribution was important to the business, and I want him to be part of the story when people talk about what I built.

He was also my brother. In the book, I write about sending him memes and jokes, and about the experience of wanting to text him after he was gone. That habit says something about how close a person can be to your everyday life. You see something funny, and you already know who you want to share it with.

He had been there in the earlier parts of my life too. I remember him helping me learn to ride a bike. Those memories belong beside the business accomplishments. The person who helped make a system work was the same person who had been my older brother long before there was a company.

After his death, I was missing both. There was a personal absence, and there was the absence of someone whose way of thinking had helped me build. It made the contribution of the people around a founder very clear to me.

When people say I built Prosper, I understand what they mean. I also know there were people whose work, ideas, and support made that possible. Chuck is one of them.

I want our story to give those contributions a place. It is an honest way to describe how a business becomes what it is, and it is a way to remember someone I loved.

In the book, Stratton writes that Chuck died by suicide. This profile centers his life, their relationship, and his contribution to the business.

## What did Carl teach you about leadership?

**Craig:** I remember Carl as someone who understood that business decisions affect people’s lives. In Built Back Bold, I describe him as a mentor and write about the attention he gave to the people around him.

What stayed with me was the way he could take someone’s future seriously. When you see ability in a person, you can do something with that observation. You can teach them, give them responsibility, help them think through a decision, or make a connection that gives them a better opportunity.

That requires time. You have to learn who the person is and what they need. Encouragement becomes more useful when it is connected to a real next step.

I see that lesson in the work we do with reps and independent owners. A person may already have the drive and the relationships. They may need a better process, a missing capability, or someone who can explain the part they have not learned yet.

I want to respect the work they have already put into their business and help them become more capable within it. That is one way to carry forward the kind of influence Carl had on me.

It also changes how I think about legacy. You can leave something behind in the way another person leads, teaches, or treats the people who work with them. That influence can keep moving through people you will never meet.

## What does “built back bold” mean to you now?

**Craig:** In the book, I question the idea that I was supposed to return to exactly who I had been before the losses. My life had changed. The people I loved were still part of me, and so was the experience of losing them. I needed to find a way to live and build with that history.

For me, boldness includes the willingness to tell the truth about what happened and where I still needed help. It includes taking responsibility for the next part of my life even when I could not see the entire path.

There can be a temptation to tell a clean success story: difficulty, lesson, recovery, achievement. My experience contains more than that. I can be proud of something we are building and still miss my brother. I can feel excited about a new idea and still wish I could tell Dad about it.

The book gave me a place to examine those things. It moves from loss and vulnerability into leadership, trust, communication, and service. That connection matters to me because what we experience personally can affect how we treat people professionally.

One chapter is called Sales as Service. Another is Leading Without a Title. Those are ideas I want to make practical. Can the customer understand the decision? Can someone on the team take useful responsibility? Can a person ask for help without feeling that their entire value is in question?

I cannot promise someone that hardship will lead to a particular reward. I can share what I learned and what helped me continue. I can tell them that my life still has room for meaningful work, love, humor, and ambition after a year that changed me.

That is the possibility I wanted to write toward: a future you can participate in building, with the whole of your experience coming with you.

## Before there was a network, there was a home. Why is that important?

**Craig:** The home has been the connection through all of it. Security, automation, solar, and storage may involve different equipment and different specialists, but they meet in the life of the same homeowner.

That person wants the house to work. They want to understand what they are spending. They want useful equipment and someone who knows how to help them. Our opportunity is to understand the pieces well enough to put together something that makes sense for that household.

That is why I have always been interested in how things connect. A customer may first know us through one service and later need another. The relationship gives us a reason to keep learning about the home and what would actually improve it.

In our security and automation work, we have used an app that lets customers interact with different parts of their homes. In our solar work, we help people understand the design and the choices around it. The tools can change. The purpose is to make something complicated easier for a person to understand and use.

There is a responsibility that comes with being invited into that decision. A house is personal. People live with what we help them choose. That should shape the care we put into the explanation and the work.

The connection is still visible in his day-to-day business conversations. In an August 2026 team meeting, Stratton described building a three-dimensional model of a customer’s property to help explain a solar and security proposal. In a September meeting, he demonstrated how home controls and solar could sit within the customer’s app.

These are small examples compared with national installation figures. They show where his attention often goes: to making an idea understandable for the person who will live with it.

## In your earlier interview, you said the hard part was recognizing what the industry needed. What did you mean?

**Craig:** Sometimes the industry needs people who can produce. Sometimes it needs people who can teach, develop a team, and bring other people into the work. Being able to recognize the moment and contribute in the right way has been important to me.

Those are different responsibilities. If I need to sell, I should be able to get close to the customer and do the work. If people need training, I have to be able to explain what I know in a way they can use. If an owner needs a better setup, the useful contribution may be putting the right relationships around that business.

A title by itself does not answer which of those things is needed today. You have to pay attention.

I still believe in leading from the front. There is credibility in knowing the work well enough to demonstrate it. There is also a point where the next step is helping someone else develop that ability. If every useful answer stays with me, then I have limited what the people around me can become.

That is part of the thinking behind Prosper now. We have experience and relationships that can help other people. The challenge is making that experience available in a practical way, so a rep, an installer, or an owner can use it to do better work.

You have to keep asking where you can contribute most. The answer changes as the business changes. Experience helps you recognize that change sooner, if you are willing to keep learning.

## You are competitive. How does that fit with your willingness to work with other companies?

**Craig:** I like to compete. I like doing the work well, and I respect people who put real effort into getting better. In that earlier interview, I talked about being willing to try, fail, and keep learning from it. There is something to respect in a person who gives the work a serious effort.

Competition can bring a lot out of you. It can show you where you are prepared and where you need to improve. It can also teach you to recognize ability in other people.

After enough years, you know that there are people who are excellent at things you need. Someone understands a local market. Someone has built a dependable installation crew. Someone has a strong financing relationship or a better way of organizing a process. Working with that person can make the result better for everyone involved.

I do not lose the value of my experience by recognizing theirs. I can use my judgment to bring the right people together, and that is valuable work too.

That is why I care more about the arrangement working than about putting our name on every piece of it. The homeowner needs the project to make sense. The rep needs a real opportunity. The installer needs a healthy business. I want to build something that respects all three.

There is still ambition in that. It takes confidence to take responsibility for bringing a group together and to keep working on the details until the group can actually deliver.

## You once expected Prosper to stay small. What changed?

**Craig:** We had an East Coast foundation, and for a long time I expected the business to remain relatively small. Then people I knew in other markets began asking us to help. They could see something useful in the setup we had built.

In the conversation with Nick, I described friends asking whether we could sell in California. My first reaction was that I did not want to go there. Then we were doing business there, and the reach kept growing through those relationships.

At the same time, the industry was going through a difficult period. We saw an opportunity to bring capabilities together when people needed more options. In a team meeting, I explained that we chose to push into more markets during that downturn.

It grew out of relationships and a need. People had businesses, customers, and skills worth preserving. We had pieces that could help. Bringing those pieces together created another way forward.

That expansion also made it clear that local knowledge matters. I told Nick I was not going to pretend to know every market. We have partners who understand their areas, and I want that experience involved in the decision.

Parts of our West Coast operation have since exceeded parts of what we built in the East. I describe the collaborative approach as wildly successful. The responsibility now is to keep strengthening the operation underneath that growth.

His account contains both excitement and strain. During the same October conversation, he describes the stress of becoming a source of support for other people in the industry. The growth brought opportunity, and it brought more people looking to him for answers.

That helps explain why the structure of the business matters so much to him. A founder can bring people together. For that effort to last, the relationships need a practical way to keep working.

## What is Prosper today?

**Craig:** Prosper is a hybrid. We have capabilities of our own, and we work with a selected group of companies whose strengths complement ours. At the core are direct relationships with owners and executives. We can talk to the people responsible for making decisions and getting things done.

Depending on the project, Prosper may provide installation, equipment, access to financing, sales, operating support, or several of those things. A partner can bring those capabilities too. We look at what the job needs and who is best positioned to provide it.

An installer may have an excellent crew and need a better flow of projects. A sales organization may already have customers and need an installation route. An independent owner may have both and need equipment purchasing, financing access, or a more workable process.

We can use our contracts and our setup when that fits. We can work through a partner’s structure when that is the better arrangement. The state, the scope of the job, the available resources, and the goal all matter.

The central idea is straightforward: assemble the capabilities around the actual need.

That is what I mean by the Prosper Method. It is the judgment behind how we put the work together. The value comes from knowing what each party can do, arranging the pieces well, and helping the people involved understand the plan.

I brought these relationships together intentionally. I could see a way for the businesses involved to be more useful to one another and better prepared for changes in the market.

## How does that help a rep and an installer at the same time?

**Craig:** A rep wants an opportunity to earn, a competitive project, and confidence that the work can be delivered. An installer needs projects it can execute well, enough margin to support the business, and a dependable way to keep its people working.

Those needs can support each other. When we bring useful volume together, we can have a stronger position in equipment purchasing and other negotiations. When we coordinate the work well, we can reduce some of the friction that makes a project harder to complete.

If those improvements create real room in the economics, there can be a benefit for both sides. The seller can get a more competitive redline, which is the agreed project price above which the seller’s compensation is calculated, subject to the deal’s terms. The installer can retain a margin that makes the job worth doing.

The underlying improvement has to be real. A lower number on a proposal does not help if the scope is incomplete or the business cannot support the installation. We have to understand what is included and what each party is responsible for.

We also want installers to have access to work from more than one source. A partner relationship can bring projects into a market even when Prosper is concentrating its own sales effort somewhere else. That gives the installer another possible source of work and gives the seller access to local capability.

Each project still stands on its own economics. The aim is a stronger arrangement that gives everyone a reason to follow through and work together again.

### What the economics can look like

Consider an illustrative 10 kW system, or 10,000 watts. If coordinated purchasing and operations genuinely reduce delivered project cost by $0.20 per watt, that creates $2,000 of additional room.

If the parties agree to pass $0.10 per watt through as a lower seller redline and retain the other $0.10 in the installation business, the seller and installer each gain $1,000 of gross economic benefit. That comparison holds the customer’s price constant and comes before other expenses or compensation splits.

The same improvement could be allocated differently, including toward a lower customer price. The arrangement determines who receives the benefit.

This is hypothetical arithmetic, not a Prosper quote, payout schedule, or measured savings result. It illustrates the economic principle: a real reduction in cost can create value that the participants agree to share.

## Who deserves the largest share of the value?

**Craig:** Whoever provides the most value should receive the most. That is how I think about it.

The contribution can take different forms. Someone brings the customer. Someone brings installation capacity. Someone arranges equipment, financing access, or the operating support that makes the project possible. The arrangement should recognize what each participant is actually contributing.

That requires clarity. People should understand the scope, the compensation, and the conditions attached to it. A productive relationship is easier to sustain when everyone can explain why it makes sense for them.

Independent businesses have to be able to remain healthy. The owner has people depending on the business. The rep has a livelihood to build. The crew has to be paid for skilled work. Those are practical realities behind every discussion about margins.

I want us to think far enough ahead that the arrangement can support the next job as well as this one. If people feel their contribution is respected and the work is worth doing, there is a better foundation for an ongoing relationship.

That is part of survivability. A company needs partners who can keep showing up. Helping the people around you build something sustainable strengthens the work you can do together.

## With several companies involved, how do you keep the customer from getting lost?

**Craig:** The customer needs a clear explanation of the project and the people responsible for it. They should understand what they are buying, whose agreement they are signing, who is installing the system, and who handles the questions that come afterward.

A flexible model requires that level of clarity. Each partner needs to know its part. The customer needs a useful contact and a clear next step. The connections between companies should make the project easier to deliver and understand.

This is also where experience matters. You learn that a project can be strong in one area and still get held up somewhere else. A capable rep needs a capable installation route. An installer needs the right information. A customer needs an explanation that matches what will actually happen.

I want us to keep improving those connections. If a homeowner has to chase people to find out who is helping them, that is something the operation needs to address.

Our goal is to build depth in local installation, electrical work, and service. We want more than one qualified option in the areas we serve, with relationships that remain useful over time. That takes work to develop market by market.

The customer’s experience is where the whole arrangement becomes real. They live with the equipment and the agreement. Our job is to put the experience behind Prosper to work for them throughout that process.

## Why make room for smaller owners to keep building their own businesses?

**Craig:** Because a smaller business can have a great deal of value. An owner may know the community, have customers who trust them, and have a team that does good work. Those strengths deserve to be taken seriously.

They may need help with the surrounding systems. That is something we can work on together. If there are sales, real volume, and the ability to support a plan, we can look at building an arrangement around it.

Some owners need a single missing piece. Others need a broader setup. It can involve proposals, installation relationships, equipment, financing access, or the process that connects them. We customize it because their businesses are different.

Where the volume supports it, a partner may also be able to develop a white label setup, using the capabilities and relationships we have assembled while continuing to build under its own brand.

I take pride in helping someone become more capable within their own business. They have already put work into building something. If our experience can help them take the next step, that is a valuable contribution.

There is room for different kinds of owners in this model. A local company can keep the qualities that make it useful to its community and gain access to capabilities that are harder to assemble alone. A larger company may need help with a specialized part of its operation. We start by understanding the actual need.

## What is behind the Solar Special Ops Crew nickname?

**Craig:** Some people have used that nickname because we can do specialized work. We have spent years developing relationships across different parts of the business, and we can put together arrangements that require more customization.

It could be finding the right installation relationship in a particular market. It could be combining equipment and financing access with the systems an owner needs. It could be helping a business connect pieces it already has.

The skill is knowing what is available, understanding how the pieces work, and recognizing when a particular combination makes sense. You have to ask enough questions to understand the problem before you try to solve it.

That capability was built intentionally. We developed the relationships and learned how to work with people at the ownership and executive level because we wanted to be useful in those situations.

I like that the nickname recognizes the contribution. People call because they believe we can help them work through something. Earning that kind of call is meaningful, and keeping that trust means continuing to do the work carefully.

## How do you lead when you are having a difficult time yourself?

**Craig:** That became a real question for me. In the book, I describe how hard it could be to concentrate, make decisions, and carry the expectations that came with being responsible for a company while I was grieving.

I had to become more honest about my capacity. People still needed leadership. I also needed support and room to be human. Trying to carry everything alone could affect both me and the people depending on me.

It gave me another reason to develop capable people around me. If the entire operation depends on one person having every answer every day, the operation has a weakness. Other people need the information, authority, and preparation to contribute.

I want a rep to understand the work well enough to make sound decisions. I want a partner to know what the arrangement requires. I want the team to have a process it can use when a situation becomes difficult. Those are practical ways to build more strength into a business.

In my earlier recorded interview, I talked about studying and working on what I needed to improve when things were difficult. That still matters. So does recognizing when someone else has the knowledge, perspective, or capacity to help.

I remain competitive. I still want us to do excellent work. My understanding of what sustains that effort has become broader. Rest, honest conversations, support, and shared responsibility have a place alongside discipline and ambition.

That is also how I want to speak to someone having a difficult season. I can take their situation seriously and still believe in their ability to move forward. We can look for the next useful step without pretending that everything is already easy.

“Not every day’s a win,” he says in the archived Dubai interview. Read alongside the book, the sentence carries the perspective of someone whose life includes both sustained ambition and experiences that could not be solved by working harder.

## What does technology change, and what still depends on people?

**Craig:** Technology can make knowledge easier to use. It can help a rep understand a proposal, help a customer visualize a system, and help a team keep track of what needs to happen next. We have been putting work into those tools and into the systems available to our partners.

The purpose is practical. A tool should help someone do the work more clearly, consistently, or efficiently. It should make useful information easier to find and help people move through a process they understand.

We have also been working with automation and AI as part of that effort. I want the experience in the business to become more accessible to the people doing the work. A newer rep should have a way to learn. An owner should be able to understand the setup. A team member should know the next step.

People still have to exercise judgment. A proposal needs to fit the actual home. A financing option needs to be explained accurately. A customer needs someone who listens and can respond when the situation does not fit the usual pattern.

That is why training remains important. Giving someone a tool creates an opportunity; helping them understand what they are doing lets them use it responsibly. The technology and the person need to develop together.

## What are you doing now, and what does the next chapter require?

**Craig:** We are strengthening the network around the work. We want more depth in installation and electrical coverage, financing options that fit the markets we serve, and systems that make it easier for partners to operate well.

Storage is an important part of the current work. We have been putting more attention on batteries and on helping people who already have solar understand their next options. That starts with what the customer has, what they want to accomplish, and whether the proposed work fits.

We are continuing to build customized arrangements for owners with real volume. We are also working on the tools and training that make our experience available to the people around us.

The long-term goal includes multiple qualified local installers and electricians in the areas we serve, with the ability to support customers over the years. That is something to build deliberately. It depends on the people, the coverage, and the agreements being in place.

Growth asks us to become more consistent. More markets mean more relationships and more details to understand. A useful next step has to be supported by the operation behind it.

I want the next chapter to give homeowners clearer choices, reps a stronger foundation for a career, and independent businesses more ways to succeed. We have accumulated a lot of experience. There is still work to do in turning that experience into something more people can use.

## What does being a father change about your definition of success?

**Craig:** It makes the future personal. You think about what your children will learn from the way you live, including the way you work and the way you treat people.

When I remember my dad, I remember specific things: his humor, his interest in learning, the way he shared an idea. That makes me think about what I am passing on in ordinary moments. The example is being built while life is happening.

In the earlier Dubai interview, when the conversation turned to achievement outside the business, my answer turned to family. That belongs in this story because a business accomplishment is one part of a life.

I want to do meaningful work and be someone the people close to me can depend on. I want my children to see that you can be ambitious and still make room for other people, that you can struggle and ask for help, and that learning continues throughout your life.

That perspective also affects how I think about Prosper. Our customers have families. The people working on the projects have families. An independent owner may have years of personal sacrifice tied up in the business we are discussing.

In a team conversation, I described our goal as leaving no family behind. That is an aspiration I want us to keep turning into practical questions. Can this person understand their options? Are we explaining the real conditions? Is there another workable route? Have we treated the people involved with care?

A company cannot solve every difficulty. It can take responsibility for the experience it creates and keep improving the parts within its control. That is meaningful work.

## What do you want Prosper to stand for?

**Craig:** I want Prosper to stand for people becoming more capable, through work they can be proud of and relationships they can depend on.

We bring experience in solar, security, home automation, sales, and business operations. We have capabilities of our own and relationships with companies whose strengths complement ours. We put those capabilities together around the needs of the customer, the project, and the businesses involved.

We believe the explanation should be clear. People should understand what they are choosing, what it costs, who is responsible, and what happens next. Clear information gives them a better foundation for their decision.

We believe contribution deserves recognition. The person who brings the customer, the crew that performs the work, and the people who arrange the equipment, financing access, and operating support all help make the project possible. The economics should recognize the value being provided and support businesses that can keep showing up.

We believe experience should be shared. A lesson learned over twenty years can help someone make a better decision today. Training, useful tools, and direct access to people who understand the work are ways to make that experience available.

We believe independence has value. A capable local owner should have opportunities to strengthen the business they have already built. Where we can provide a missing piece or a better arrangement, we want to be useful.

We believe follow-through matters. The promise, the agreement, the installation, and the support should connect. When something goes wrong, the work includes understanding it and helping identify the next step.

And we believe people bring their whole lives with them. The homeowner, the rep, the installer, the owner, and the person answering the phone all have responsibilities and experiences beyond the transaction. Respecting that should affect how we communicate and how carefully we do the work.

These are the standards I want us to keep practicing. Our history gives us experience. Our daily decisions determine whether that experience becomes something other people can rely on.

That is what I want the name Prosper to mean.

## What would you say to someone who is still at their own beginning?

**Craig:** Give yourself permission to learn while you are doing the work. I did not begin with the relationships, knowledge, and confidence I have today. Those developed through years of effort and through people who helped me.

If speaking is difficult, keep giving yourself opportunities to develop your voice. If you are new to the business, ask questions and get close enough to understand what the work requires. If something has changed your life, you do not have to pretend it has not happened before you can take another step.

Find people you can learn from. Let someone help you with the part you do not yet understand. When you become more capable, make that experience available to someone else.

I think about how much I received from the people in my life. Dad gave me curiosity and a love of learning. Eden made me think more deeply about care and the burdens people carry. Chuck gave me brotherhood, laughter, and a way of understanding systems that helped the business. Carl gave me an example of taking people and their futures seriously.

I carry those people into what I do next. Their influence is still useful. It still asks something of me.

You may be starting with a small business, a new career, a difficult season, or an idea that other people cannot quite see yet. Begin with what you can do honestly and well. Keep learning enough to take the next responsibility. Build relationships that can support real work.

I am proud of what Prosper has accomplished. I also see the people whose next chapter is still taking shape: the rep learning the work, the owner trying to strengthen a company, the homeowner trying to make a decision, the person wondering what they can build after life changes.

I want the experience behind Prosper to be useful to them.

Experience teaches you where to lead, where to partner, and how to build something that lasts.

In the dedication to his father, Stratton promises to keep learning, leading, and laughing. It is a fitting place to return after the discussion of companies, careers, economics, and growth.

The promise holds the person and the work together. There is ambition in it, and affection. There is room for responsibility and for the humor he remembers from home.

The Prosper Method will be tested in the ordinary work ahead: a clear explanation, a fair arrangement, a team prepared to act, a customer who knows who will help. That is where a founder’s statement becomes something people can experience for themselves.
