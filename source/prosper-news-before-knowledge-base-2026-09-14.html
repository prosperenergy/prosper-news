Mini nuclear is a promise. Your roof is a power plant.

The energy debate has spent enough time admiring promises. Families pay actual bills. A technology earns its place by delivering useful electricity, at an acceptable cost, on a schedule that matters.

Solar and batteries deserve to lead the next chapter of American energy. Building a household’s energy plan around the promise of a future small modular reactor is the wrong bet.

Small modular reactors deserve the same test as any proposed power plant: credible financing, licensing, construction, delivered electricity, and an honest account of cost and timing. A reactor announcement cannot diagnose an inverter fault, improve the solar already on your roof, or give your home backup power.

That is Prosper’s position. We are prepared to argue it plainly: build more solar, pair it with intelligently designed storage, improve the homes using that power, and fix the grid connections that hold good projects back. Give people more control over the energy they produce and buy.

Nuclear advocates are entitled to make their case. They should have to make it with delivered costs, completed projects, and credible alternatives for the years before a reactor opens. An impressive rendering cannot power a house. A revised completion date cannot lower this month’s bill.

Solar and batteries have a history of measurable technological progress behind them. That is why we back them now—and expect them to remain central to the energy system ahead. The evidence deserves a much stronger conclusion than another vague declaration that every option is equally attractive.

These figures describe different things. They are evidence about cost and execution; they are not a like-for-like price comparison between a solar farm, a household installation, and a nuclear plant.

1. Solar’s environmental case survives a full accounting

A photovoltaic cell converts absorbed light into electrical energy in a semiconductor. An inverter makes that electricity usable by a home or the grid. The panel needs no fuel combustion and no steam turbine to generate power. [3]

Manufacturing still matters. Silicon processing, metals, glass, transport, installation, and equipment replacement all carry environmental costs. A serious assessment includes those stages and end-of-life treatment, then divides their impacts by the electricity the system produces over its lifetime.

The International Energy Agency’s PV programme does exactly that. Its 2024 assessment found non-renewable energy payback times of roughly 0.8–1.2 years across four photovoltaic technologies under its specified European residential-system assumptions. The calculation assumes a 30-year panel service life, a 15-year inverter life, and a particular solar resource. It is an energy accounting result, not a financial payback promise for an American homeowner. [4]

That finding matters because solar’s manufacturing burden is incurred largely at the beginning. Years of subsequent electricity production spread that burden over a growing output. Sunlight, system performance, equipment lifetime, and the manufacturing electricity mix affect the result.

The same evidence establishes an essential fact: nuclear is also a low-carbon electricity source. In this dataset, its median life-cycle emissions are lower than solar’s. Solar’s case against new nuclear therefore depends heavily on how much useful clean energy can be delivered, at what cost, and how soon.

That is a more demanding test than a slogan. It is also the test that matters for a real energy decision.

2. Follow the money. Solar and storage have earned their advantage.

IRENA reports that new utility-scale solar averaged $44/MWh globally in 2025, down 89% since 2010 but flat against 2024. Its estimate for installed four-hour utility batteries was about $140/kWh, roughly 95% below 2010. [1]

Those figures measure different services. A solar-generation cost is not a complete, round-the-clock electricity-system cost. A utility battery price is not an installed Powerwall quote. What matters is the direction of the record: these technologies have already made expensive energy services dramatically more affordable.

Battery history strengthens the case. In a 2021 Energy & Environmental Science study, Micah Ziegler and Jessika Trancik reconstructed lithium-ion price trends from underlying sources. They found an approximately 97% fall in the real price of cells per unit of energy capacity since commercial introduction in 1991, over the period studied. That is cell-level history, not a forecast or a residential-system discount. [19]

A companion study by Ziegler, Juhyun Song, and Trancik examined the mechanisms. Public and private research and development accounted for the largest share of the estimated cost decline in their analysis; economies of scale also contributed. Better chemistry and better engineering mattered. [20]

This is the economic advantage of technologies that can improve through repeated manufacturing, materials research, and broad deployment. Each installation does not require inventing a new construction industry around one enormous project.

For homeowners, the calculation comes back to the house: the bill, production, usage timing, financing, export compensation, and the value of backup. A good solar-and-storage proposal makes those numbers visible. It gives the family something concrete to evaluate.

Our view is that an energy strategy should reward this record of delivered improvement. Solar and batteries have earned serious deployment, serious infrastructure investment, and a leading place in the plan.

3. Nuclear’s promises need to survive contact with its history

Start with Vogtle. The Georgia expansion gives us something more useful than a nuclear sales pitch: original expectations and an operating project whose history can be examined.

Vogtle expansion Original expectation Actual outcome or reported estimate
Unit 3 commercial operation 2016 July 2023
Unit 4 commercial operation 2017 April 2024
Total expansion cost $14 billion More than $30 billion, as reported by EIA in May 2024

Source: EIA, May 1, 2024. These are historical project figures, not a cost forecast for every future reactor. [7]

Two reactors. Roughly seven years beyond their originally expected operating dates. A reported price tag exceeding twice the original expectation. That is an extraordinary result to wave away while insisting nuclear should be the centerpiece of an affordability strategy. [7]

Vogtle now supplies useful low-carbon power. Families and policymakers still have every reason to demand accountability for the path taken to get there.

The problem has a longer history. A 2020 Joule study by Philip Eash-Gates and colleagues examined 50 years of U.S. nuclear construction data. It found that repeated designs had not reliably delivered the expected cost improvements. Engineering, coordination, changing site conditions, and other construction processes helped explain rising costs; changing safety rules were only part of the story. The researchers identified ways better design and factory production might improve future results. [8]

The lesson is uncomfortable for nuclear advocates: a sophisticated reactor design does not, by itself, establish that a project can be delivered affordably. Construction organisation, financing, and execution are part of the technology’s real performance.

An industry asking for another major commitment should prove it can control these risks. Optimism is not a cost-control system.

Vogtle cannot tell us the price of every reactor in every country. It can tell American decision-makers why “this time will be different” deserves hard questions. Price the project honestly. Include financing. Show the delivery schedule. Compare it with a realistic solar, storage, and grid portfolio. Then explain who carries the overruns. That is the standard new nuclear should face.

4. A power plant on paper does nothing for today’s problem

A project can have excellent lifetime emissions and still be a weak response to an urgent problem if its output arrives much later than competing options.

The relevant counterfactual is straightforward: what supplies electricity while the project is being built? If earlier solar generation would have displaced fossil generation, delay has an emissions cost. If it would have been curtailed or displaced another clean source, the benefit is smaller. That assessment needs hourly grid conditions, realistic completion dates, and an achievable alternative portfolio.

Solar’s modularity creates opportunities to add useful capacity in stages. The U.S. development pipeline demonstrates the scale of that opportunity: EIA’s February 2026 outlook included 43.4 GW of solar and 24 GW of battery power capacity. [2]

Solar also faces serious delays. Berkeley Lab’s 2026 queue study reports a median of more than five years from interconnection request to commercial operation for projects built in 2025 in regions with available data. Its queue covers multiple technologies, and many proposed projects are withdrawn. [9]

That is an argument for getting on with the work: transmission, connection studies, permitting, and execution. Spend effort removing the obstacles to useful deployment. A slow connection process is a problem to fix, not a reason to abandon technologies with a compelling cost and manufacturing record.

The latest deployment evidence is stronger than a pipeline announcement. EIA reported on August 7 that operators added 8.3 GW of utility-scale batteries in the first half of 2026, taking the operating fleet from 43.6 GW at the end of 2025 to nearly 52 GW by June. Another 14 GW was planned for the second half of 2026. Those future additions remain plans; the first-half additions are reported installations. These figures update the battery outlook discussed above without turning the February chart into a record of completed projects. [21]

Berkeley Lab’s latest queue data also show why execution matters: 773 GW of solar and 749 GW of storage were seeking transmission connections at the end of 2025. Both totals declined from the previous year, by 19% and 16% respectively. A queue entry does not establish that a project is financed, built, or ready to supply power. These figures exclude behind-the-meter and distribution-connected projects; those systems can still require local utility approval. [9]

5. “The sun goes down” is the beginning of the design brief

Yes, the sun goes down. That is why storage belongs in this argument. Solar produces during daylight and varies with weather. Batteries shift some of that production to a later hour, with energy losses along the way. Storage must be evaluated in two dimensions: power, measured in kilowatts, and energy, measured in kilowatt-hours. The first governs how much equipment it can serve at once; the second helps determine how long it can serve it. [10]

A battery described as providing four hours at rated power does not automatically cover a prolonged winter shortfall. Useful reliability comes from the whole system: different generation resources, storage duration, transmission, demand flexibility, reserves, and controls.

Those controls are advancing. The Department of Energy’s UNIFI programme describes grid-forming inverters that can establish voltage and frequency, helping inverter-based resources support a stable grid. These are engineered capabilities that require suitable equipment and coordinated operation. They are not features to assume in every existing solar installation. [11]

There is also credible evidence for retaining firm clean resources. A 2018 Joule study by Nestor Sepulveda and colleagues examined nearly 1,000 modelling cases. Access to firm low-carbon options reduced costs by 10–62% in its fully decarbonised cases. Nuclear was one of several firm options studied. [12]

“Firm power has value” does not establish that the next nuclear megaproject is the best way to buy it. The relevant test is the cost and reliability of the whole portfolio.

Our conclusion is that solar should be the leading investment priority within a reliable portfolio. Existing nuclear plants can contribute valuable clean power, and replacing their output with fossil generation can undermine the purpose of decarbonisation. That is a different decision from committing to an expensive new reactor.

Why we expect solar and storage to keep leading

The strongest forward-looking case connects physical capability with demonstrated improvement. Solar converts a widespread resource into electricity. Storage expands when that electricity can be used. Manufacturing experience, chemistry, installation practice, and better controls create several routes to make the combination more useful.

In a 2022 Joule paper, Rupert Way and colleagues used probabilistic technology forecasts calibrated against historical data. Their modelled rapid clean-energy transition produced trillions in potential net savings compared with continued fossil-fuel dependence. Those results depend on the scenarios; they strengthen the case for taking technological progress seriously when choosing what to build. [6]

IRENA’s 2026 outlook projects further solar-plus-battery cost reductions. At high-quality sites, its modelled 95%-reliability hybrid electricity cost falls about 30% by 2030 and 40% by 2035. That benchmark is not complete grid reliability. The report explicitly describes projections as uncertain and warns that financing, supply chains, and material prices can interrupt progress. [1]

Our conviction does not require prices to fall every quarter. It rests on a demonstrated ability to improve—and on equipment people can already put to work.

No honest paper proves what every electricity market will choose decades from now. The record does support a forceful decision today: give solar and storage the leading role, and keep investing in the engineering that extends their reach. We see far more reason to build on that progress than to reorganise our energy future around nuclear construction promises.

6. Nuclear’s obligations continue after the ribbon-cutting

Photovoltaics have an operational advantage that receives too little attention: they generate electricity without a thermal cooling cycle.

Jordan Macknick and colleagues’ review in Environmental Research Letters found that non-thermal renewables, including PV and wind, have the lowest operational water-consumption factors. The study also found that cooling-system design can matter more than fuel category. Water withdrawn and water consumed are different measures; a single universal “nuclear water use” number would obscure that distinction. [13]

For nuclear power, waste creates a separate obligation. Used fuel remains hot and radioactive and requires managed handling, shielding, storage, and eventual disposal. The NRC describes long-lived hazards that require protection over very long periods. It also regards properly managed spent-fuel pools and dry casks as protective of public health and the environment. [14]

This is a lasting responsibility, rather than evidence that a nearby operating reactor is automatically harming its neighbours. The costs and institutions needed to maintain that responsibility belong in any honest nuclear assessment.

Small modular reactors do not justify skipping the question. A 2022 PNAS study by Lindsay Krall and colleagues identified increased or more complicated waste streams in the designs it examined. A separate Argonne-led analysis reached more favourable conclusions for its selected designs. Designs, metrics, and assumptions differ. A claim that every small reactor either solves or worsens the waste problem would exceed that evidence. [15][16]

Solar also needs responsible end-of-life management. Its glass, metals, semiconductor materials, and associated batteries require collection and appropriate processing. [17] Ground-mounted projects require careful land-use decisions. Rooftop systems can use already-built surfaces, but neither that advantage nor lower operating water use makes the whole supply chain impact-free.

Our preference remains solar: build around its manageable design and siting challenges while avoiding additional commitments to complex nuclear fuel-cycle obligations wherever a dependable, affordable alternative can do the job.

7. Give households a stake in their own power

For most families, nuclear power is an upstream utility decision. Solar can become a decision about the home itself: how much electricity to produce, when to use it, and whether storing some of it improves the household’s economics or backup capability.

That control matters. But the proposal has to fit the house.

A strong assessment starts with a year of electricity usage, the actual rate structure, the site’s solar resource, and the condition of the installation area. It should explain expected production, equipment assumptions, financing costs where relevant, export compensation, and what remains on the utility bill.

For storage, it should identify the loads to support, usable energy, output limits, and the conditions under which backup operates. Grid-connected solar alone generally shuts down during an outage; continuing to serve the home requires equipment designed for that purpose. The Department of Energy explains how solar and storage can provide resilience when appropriately configured. [10][18]

Already have panels? Start by checking production and the bill. Adding storage should solve an identified problem: storing surplus generation for later use, responding to a time-dependent rate, or supporting selected loads during an outage. The system design decides which benefits are realistic.

A bill review and site audit turn a broad energy argument into a decision a homeowner can actually evaluate. Use Prosper’s three-step solar and storage checklist to prepare for that conversation.

8. Build solar. Add storage. Stop treating nuclear promises as a plan.

We want an energy future that delivers for the people paying for it. That means cleaner electricity, dependable service, defensible costs, and more household control.

Solar and batteries are the strongest starting point for that future. The science supports their environmental value. The historical cost record shows extraordinary progress. Their modular nature creates opportunities to deploy and improve in stages. Proper engineering makes the combination more useful than a panel considered in isolation.

New nuclear megaprojects bring a very different proposition: concentrated construction risk, major financing exposure, and long-term fuel-cycle obligations. We oppose making that proposition the default answer to near-term affordability. A future reactor design should earn its place through demonstrated delivery, not receive it because its announcement sounds impressive.

Our priorities are direct:

  1. Build solar where it delivers useful power and sound economics.
  2. Add storage where it improves timing, reliability, or household value.
  3. Improve home efficiency so families need less electricity in the first place.
  4. Expand the grid, controls, and connections that let clean resources work together.
  5. Make every competing proposal prove its total cost and delivery case.

That is an aggressive position because the stakes justify one. Money and time committed to an underperforming project are money and time unavailable for another approach. Households deserve an energy strategy that treats both as valuable.

Prosper backs solar and batteries now, and we believe they should lead the decades ahead. Build on the progress. Put the engineering to work. Give families more control.
